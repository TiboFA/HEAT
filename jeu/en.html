December 2015: the Paris Agreement has just been signed. You have seventeen five-year turns, until 2100.

One objection comes up every time climate is discussed: “it's pointless anyway.” This game puts it to the test. Take the side that wants to constrain, or the side that wants to buy time — both are playable, both have real arguments, and in the end physics decides.

How a game is judged
The world emits 35.5 GtCO₂ a year in 2015, and without climate policy it will emit 57 in 2100. Your game is judged on two dated milestones:

★ The peak. The year emissions stop rising. A game with no action never peaks.
★ The threshold. The year the world drops below 20 GtCO₂ a year.

What counts is not crossing the milestones but in which year. A yes/no target would say that missing the mark wipes out the effort; physics says the opposite. Temperature depends on cumulative CO₂: every five-year slice gained removes gigatonnes from the total, including after the threshold is crossed, and even if it never is. The 2100 temperature is still shown — but as a consequence, not as the goal.

The wait-and-see side scores symmetrically: on the delay of both milestones, and on their never being reached.

The rules, in detail
A power budget per turn, and not all levers cost the same. Each card shows its cost, from 1 to 3.5 points in steps of 0.5 — the card says so: ⚖ 1.5 pt. Thirty-eight of the 112 levers are at the minimum (1 point), forty-one are heavy (2 points or more) and six are extreme (3 points or more): those alone shift the trajectory, swing world opinion or lower the caps. The cost is not decreed, it is measured by replaying each lever alone on a standard game state. Your budget is not fixed: it follows your credibility, between 3 points at the lowest and 6 at the highest (4.5 at neutral credibility). Being believed widens what you can afford in a turn; being discredited narrows it. So each turn you choose between one or two big moves and several small ones. The opponent pays in the same currency; its budget is set by the difficulty level and, like yours, follows its credibility. Click a card in your hand, then a bloc on the world map. A global card has no target to pick: one click commits it. The i at the top of each card opens its full sheet: mechanism, dated real-world anchor, limit, counter-lever, and expected return bloc by bloc.

Nothing is played until you confirm the turn. The levers you commit stack up in the “your turn” strip above the map: you can remove one at any time (× on the lever, or Ctrl+Z), reset everything, and always see what the plan should produce — 2100 projection, score, cost, and gauge by gauge on each bloc. Bets are only settled on confirmation: the preview assumes success.

112 levers from the catalogue, seven of which deliver far less than they promise and cost you credibility when the gap shows — nothing on the card sets them apart, only the sheet says so. You only see twelve at a time: your doctrine decides which. A global lever can be played only once, a bloc lever on three blocs at most — but a lever is only used up by an effect obtained: an attempt that fails, or a measure the opponent repeals, makes it available again. It then carries the mark ↻ replayable.

Four gauges per bloc, in the order of the bars: constraint (what brings emissions down), actual support (slow, cannot be countered), perceived support (volatile, it is what allows measures), friction (above 55, a measure is repealed).

Credibility is a ratio, not a gauge. Nobody is believed in absolute terms: you are believed rather than the other side. So the number that counts is not your level but the gap, shown next to it at the top of the screen. Whatever moves it moves both sides: discrediting the opponent credits you with half of what you take from them, being discredited credits them as much. You have no repair lever — you climb back when the other side gets caught. The sum is not conserved: one loses 14, the other gains 7, and an exchange of accusations damages both sides.
It weighs on your bets, up to 10 points of probability, and on your power budget for the turn, between 3 and 6 points. Above all it sets the speed at which perceived support catches up with actual support: 5% per turn if the opponent has the floor, 25% if you do. It is the speed at which the truth gets out.
It also moves without anyone playing it. Every four turns, the trajectory decides: it has fallen, you are believed; it stalls, the other side is. A backlash proves the wait-and-see side right, the emissions peak proves the active side right, a missed net-zero deadline costs dearly, and a lever that promises more than it delivers is paid for in credibility. Finally, controversy fades: every turn, both credibilities drift back a little towards 50. A lead cannot be banked, it has to be maintained.

Events only count if they are attributed. Attributing costs 1 attention and uses no power. Disasters become more frequent as the world warms — twice as often at 1.5 °C, three times at 2 °C — and three tipping events only appear beyond a threshold: crops lost on several continents (1.6 °C), climate exodus (1.8 °C), tipping point (2.0 °C). No event adds constraint: it moves opinion, actual support or friction, never the policies themselves.

The climate fits in one line: temperature = 1.0 + 0.00058 × CO₂ cumulated since 2015. The coefficient, 0.58 °C per thousand gigatonnes, is above the IPCC central estimate for CO₂ alone (0.45 °C, likely range 0.27 to 0.63). It can be read as roughly including the warming from other gases — methane, nitrous oxide — which the game does not model separately. It is a deliberate simplification, not a hidden exaggeration. The game runs to 2100: the temperature shown is not an extrapolation, it is the one your game reaches. With no action at all, it ends at 3.48 °C, with emissions rising from 35.5 to about 57 GtCO₂/yr. The real world is on 2.70 °C — the grey line on the chart. That is the Climate Action Tracker's current-policies projection (November 2026, up 0.1 °C in a year); the UN Environment Programme gives 2.8 °C. COP31, in Antalya, ended without an agreement. A well-played game lands between 2.0 and 2.5 °C: action pays off handsomely, and 1.5 °C has been out of reach since 2015.

Growth and reduction are annual rates. A bloc left alone follows its trend, damped over the century; a bloc brought to 100 constraint sees its emissions fall by 5.5% a year. A turn applies five years of that regime: constraint installed early therefore pays far more than the same constraint installed late.
